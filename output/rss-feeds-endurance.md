# Why RSS Feeds Never Died
*Researched 2026-08-19 · 3 sources*

## Executive Summary
Despite the rise of social media algorithms and the closure of high-profile aggregators like Google Reader in 2013, RSS (Really Simple Syndication) has endured as a vital infrastructure for the open web. It remains a preferred tool for users who value autonomy, chronological content delivery, and privacy over platform-curated feeds.

**Key takeaways**
- RSS provides a user-controlled, chronological alternative to opaque algorithmic content ranking.
- It is platform-agnostic, allowing users to switch between reader apps without losing their subscriptions.
- The protocol remains the invisible backbone of modern media, including the entire podcasting ecosystem.
- Modern readers have evolved to support sites that lack native RSS by monitoring them for updates.

## Background
RSS is a standardized format for distributing web content updates. Originally designed to help users track changes on their favorite websites without visiting each one manually, it evolved into a fundamental part of the internet's "open" era. While its popularity waned as social media giants incentivized closed, platform-centric consumption, the core technology never vanished.

## Key Findings
### Autonomy vs. Algorithmic Control
RSS empowers users to curate their own information diet [1, 2]. Unlike social media platforms that use opaque algorithms to decide what content a user sees—often prioritizing engagement or sponsored posts—RSS delivers content chronologically and directly [1, 2]. This allows users to follow niche sites, local news, and blogs without the "noise" or manipulation of platform-driven discovery [2, 3].

### Portability and Standards
A primary reason for its endurance is that RSS is an open, platform-agnostic standard [2, 3]. Because most readers allow users to import and export subscription lists (typically via OPML files), users are not locked into any single ecosystem [2]. If a preferred app shuts down, the user can move their entire library to a new reader in minutes [2].

### Ubiquity and Modern Evolution
While often overlooked in general web discourse, RSS is the fundamental protocol behind the global podcasting ecosystem [1, 2]. Furthermore, while social media platforms have made "the algorithm" the center of user experience, RSS has quietly seen a resurgence as a tool to bypass those very systems [3]. Modern readers have adapted by supporting newsletters, YouTube, and even sites that don't explicitly offer feeds by monitoring them for new activity [1, 3].

## What This Means
For the average reader, RSS represents a "power user" tool for reclaiming the internet from centralized feeds. By removing the algorithmic middleman, readers can ensure they don't miss content from their preferred sources, regardless of how rarely or frequently those sites update.
